The roof, and what fits on it

The roof is a metal-sheet shed: a 60 m ridge with two 20 m slopes facing east and west, tilted 7°. After setbacks and two skylight strips per slope, 1,983 m² is usable. The plant uses only part of it, because the size is set by the smallest of three limits: the roof, the grid rules, and the factory's daytime load.

Why 17 modules? A string's open-circuit voltage rises as it gets cold. At the assumed record low of 3.4 °C, 17 modules give 931.8 V; 18 would give 986.7 V, only 1.3% under the 1,000 V connector limit.

Why this size? Roof limit 407 kWp, grid-rule limit 332 kW, load limit 321 kWp. The smallest wins, then rounds down to whole strings split equally between the slopes.

Wind. The code gives 50 m/s for Kolkata. Flush modules weigh only 0.12 kN/m², so the real question is clamp pull-out and purlin strength. That needs the actual roof.

What the plant makes

PVsyst predicts 409,153 kWh a year, or 1,331 kWh per kWp, with a performance ratio of 84.9%. Orienting the modules east and west costs about 6% against the best single tilt, but it spreads output across the day, which suits a two-shift factory.

Connecting to the grid

The factory takes power at 11 kV through its own transformer. WBSEDCL's procedures let an HT consumer connect its plant at its low-voltage bus, as long as the net meter sits on the high-voltage side.

Every 2 years above 170 kW

An open size question. The utility document pairs 170 kW with 200 kVA, a power factor of about 0.85. If the capacity cap means kWp at that ratio, a 350 kVA contract demand allows 297.5 kW, and this plant (307.4 kWp) would be 9.9 kWp over. The fix would be 28 strings, 269 kWp. The documents do not say which reading is right, so the larger size is kept and the question is flagged.

RESCO calculator

A RESCO pays for the plant and sells the power to the factory at a discount to the grid. Change the inputs: it runs the same arithmetic as the Python model in the repository.

Inputs

Cumulative cash of the RESCO

The grid price is the WBSEDCL 11 kV industrial energy charge, Rate E(B), about ₹7.06 per kWh; the fuel-cost adjustment (MVCA) is extra and not included. Capex comes from two installer pages. Maintenance cost, escalation and the discount are assumed. The calculation ignores tax, loans and inverter replacement, and gives the client no credit for lower demand charges (₹384 per kVA per month).

What to take from it

At about ₹7 per kWh, a RESCO that discounts 20 to 30% earns roughly 17 to 19% a year on its money (no tax or loan), and the factory saves ₹5 to ₹8 lakh in year one with no capex. The biggest risks are the capex (installer pages, not a quote), the real yield (soiling and shading are not yet modelled) and how the capacity cap is read. If the factory is on the time-of-day scheme, the return is about 19.0% instead of 19.4%.

Before it is switched on

The repository has a commissioning checklist with expected readings worked out from the design: a healthy 17-module string should read about 882 V open-circuit at 25 °C cell temperature, and 836 V at 45 °C. A one-page safety note covers the three real hazards on this roof: falls through skylights, strings that cannot be switched off in daylight, and DC fire.
